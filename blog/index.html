I learn all of my difficult content with mindmaps. When I tell people this, they will oftentimes look at me as if I have sprouted a set of horns. I want to briefly explain why I do this, and then encourage you to try it as well. Bulleted list today so we can get to the point more quickly.

Framing

I’d first like to frame this discussion by going over a few key facts about how learning works:

  • Learning happens in your brain. Whatever notes you take should help the learning that’s going on in your head—if your notes don’t do this, then they are irrelevant. This implies that there is no privileged way of taking notes: you just need to activate the right processes in your brain, so whatever does that best is what you should do. (Hint: linear note-taking does this super shittily.)

  • There are different orders of learning. Clearly, memorizing a fact is, in some sense, a “lower” kind of learning compared to understanding a fact, which is “lower” compared to being able to tell whether or not the fact is important or how it relates to other things, and all of these are “lower” than being able to create something new based off of that fact. These “orders” of learning are real, and in learning we should try to activate the highest orders possible. Bloom’s Revised Taxonomy gives a nice visual of this:

  • Mastery has common features. Naively, it might seem like being a master in any given skill, be it mental or physical, is specific to that discipline—after all, why should a chess grandmaster use the same learning techniques that a boxing champion uses? But when scientists study masters in any given discipline, it turns out that there are commonalities to how they learn, e.g. masters “chunk” or operate within a “schema”. Since there is a known set of mental tactics that the best in any discipline use when learning, we should also use these tactics.

A Case Study in Confidence

November 26, 2026 at 4:15:55 PM PST · 6 min read · Substack

Follow GOATs to find salt

[This is blog post 15 out of 30 in my thirty-day blogging challenge, where I post something every day for thirty days.]

Ancient hunters used the intrinsic behavior of geophagy in ungulates like goats, deer, and bison to find salt reserves in unexplored territory. In other words, they followed goats to find salt. Similarly, I study the greatest of all time to try and find commonalities between them. I follow GOATs to find salt—salt being the secret sauce that the greatest people in a given domain have that the average person doesn’t.

I’ve recently come across a very interesting pattern among some of the most truly talented humans to ever live, each of whom is the undisputed best in their domain, and I’d like to briefly tell you about it. The greats are: Magnus Carlsen (the greatest chess player of all time), Lionel Messi (the greatest soccer player of all time), Roger Gracie (the greatest Brazilian jiu-jitsu competitor of all time), and Max Verstappen (the greatest F1 racer of all time). I’d recommend watching interviews of them to get a sense of the commonality I’m talking about.

What I noticed is that each of them has a sort of “quiet confidence” that manifests as being very undefensive and “comfortable in their own skin”. You can condense this by saying that they’re unaffected1 and undefensive. I think this arises from a combination of low neuroticism and an internal standard of confidence.

Low neuroticism means that they seem to have low amounts of negative emotion. All of them appear to be very chilled out and non-anxious people. This is in contrast to tons of high performers, who use anxiety to fuel their every action (e.g. Garry Kasparov, Michael Phelps, and Kobe Bryant).

An internal standard of confidence means they care about their own opinion much more than others’. That is, they judge their own abilities without projecting what they think others would think of them onto them. This is in opposition to other great athletes, like Michael Jordan or Gordon Ryan, who are insecure overachievers. Public perception greatly affects their mental well-being, despite the fact that they are arguably the greatest of all time in what they do. Note that this dichotomy implies that having bountiful evidence of exceptional ability, in and of itself, won’t make you low-anxiety about performing well. So maybe it’s not a problem to be anxious before performing, as some of the greatest in history were this way as well.

The chill nature of the aforementioned greats isn’t to say that they are emotionless. They still, for example, sometimes get nervous before competing and care about their public image. For instance, Roger Gracie was so nervous before his match with Buchecha that he had to punch his legs to keep them from collapsing due to adrenaline, and Carlsen, Verstappen, and Messi are famous for their sometimes explosive anger while in their respective games. Their quiet confidence appears to largely stem from the fact that they seem to separate their public image from their self-image. This reduces your anxiety, in both general life and in competition, as you don’t have to worry about what others will think about you if you fail.

One concrete way to become more undefensive and unaffected is to simply notice all of your affectations and defensive behaviors and stop doing them. This is better than actively trying to seem unbothered—we all know that trying to act nonchalant actually just equates to looking like an insecure tryhard. Reducing your chalantness is the way to go if you want to be nonchalant, just like reducing defensiveness and affectations is the way to go if you want to be undefensive and unaffected. Concretely, here are some defensive behaviors or affectations that you might have and want to consider dropping:

  • Defensiveness:

    • Over-explaining yourself—just state your opinion and wait in silence.

    • Qualifying your failures, e.g. “Yeah I did bad on that test but only because my dog was undergoing chemotherapy and I got bad sleep the night before and my pencil was dull and ...”. Just say something like “Yeah, I did bad on that test, I had some stuff in my life going on and I let it affect me,” and then move on with your life.

    • Excusing yourself in advance, e.g. “Oh my gosh I’m so rusty, I don’t know if I can do well on this test.” Just do the test, man.

  • Affectations:

    • Laughing when things aren’t funny. Read the room—you don’t need to be a somber person in the name of unaffectedness—but try not to be insincere.

    • Saying sorry when you don’t even get close to bumping into someone. You can literally pass someone three feet away from you and they will say “sorry”, as if they just rammed headfirst into your chest. Don’t do this. It’s just a social pleasantry that is irrelevant in most circumstances.

    • Not accepting compliments. Instead of explaining in detail why a compliment doesn’t apply to you, just say something like “Thanks, I appreciate that,” and move on.

    • Generally being performative. Everyone can tell that you’re doing this—you’re not slick. If you, for example, call yourself an “idiot” or “dumb” and then constantly humblebrag about academics, then stop. If you constantly talk about how misunderstood and lonely you feel, perhaps coupled with the fact that you aren’t like other guys or that you’re a tortured genius or blah blah whatever, just stop.

    • Faking emotions when unnecessary, e.g. being overly enthusiastic just because you think you need to. There tends to be much more social pressure for girls to fake emotions like this, so I understand that this might sound difficult, but I’d strongly recommend picking up the habit of being genuine with your emotional presentation.

    • Pretending not to know that something is impressive so you can get people to compliment you on it. I usually see this happen with intelligence.

Some of these might seem silly as behaviors to actively drop, but I tend to believe that small actions shape your character over time. If you baby step your way away from unnecessary learned social niceties, over time you’ll become much more original and unaffected. If you learn to sit with the discomfort of people not knowing that despite your bad score on the test you’re actually super smart and it was a fluke and you were tired and blah blah blah, then over time you won’t care as much what others think about your intelligence.

I skew less defensive and affected in general, but dropping the few behaviors that I had made me noticeably more chill, undefensive, and unaffected. If you tend toward more self-consciousness and anxiety, it might work even better for you. If you decide to try this, let me know how it goes!

1

In the literal sense of being “without affectation”, with an affectation being a behavior that is unnatural to your personality.

Concrete Ways to Not Procrastinate and Get Things Done

November 25, 2026 at 5:02:20 PM PST · 10 min read · Substack

Get ’er done!

[This is blog post 14 out of 30 in my thirty-day blogging challenge, where I post something every day for thirty days.]

I spend a lot of time thinking about how to be more productive. I believe I have to think about meta-productivity more than other people have to, as I seem to find it much, much harder than others do to get myself to do things that I don’t like. I also find that I can be more susceptible to distractions compared to others. So, hopefully someone finds these tips useful! (Trust that these are all battle-tested--doing these things has taken me from literally minutes of poor focus to hours upon hours of impeccable focus.)

Note: productivity is a pretty individualized thing. Try to see the forest instead of the trees here. If there are super specific things that you feel are dumb or don’t work, try them, and if they still feel dumb and don’t work then just don’t do them. But be wary in dismissing a more central-seeming item--I have rarely seen productive people differ on the central tenets of productivity.

First, the definition of productivity is output per unit time. This output is defined in reference to a goal, like doing well on a test or getting something done at work. “Output” is a vague term, but practically, it’s what you produce by working, and you can always impose some sort of reasonable quantitative metric on it. For example, if you’re a writer, you can use words written, or if you’re a student, you can use pages read, etc. If you treat the metric of success in a reward-hacking way, you’ll get nothing out of it--for example, you can “read” tons of pages of a textbook if you move super fast without learning anything. So try to choose a metric that measures what you want to actually have happen, and then don’t reward hack.

Key ideas

  • Track the amount of time that you’re working. This is essential for having an accurate view of how much time something actually takes and to see how much you’re actually working. You should stop the timer when you’re not actively on task, e.g. if you’re in the bathroom or if someone comes by and talks to you. If you’ve never done this before, you will be utterly astonished by how little you actually work.

  • Prioritize. Things aren’t of equal importance. If you specify a goal, you can always impose a partial order on the set of actions you need to take in reference to that goal. Thus there is always a most important todo to work on to get closer to your goal. You should bias toward constantly doing the most important thing on your list, and batching the unimportant things on your list for times when you’re less mentally sharp.

  • Timeblock. This is simple, but it works. Have a concrete todo, or ordered set of todos, in your mind, and then set a timer for X hours to work on them. X should eventually get to four hours or more at a time, excluding bathroom breaks.

  • Make simple todo lists. You should just have a few things on this, like “complete X project” or “read Y book”, etc. Complicated or long todo lists are another form of procrastination and are a symptom of poor prioritization.

  • Avoid context switching. Work on one thing for a long period of time and do not do anything else. You will lose focus if you constantly context switch, and doing so will degrade your ability to focus long-term. This actually happened to me when I really got into vibe-coding for a few weeks--my long-term focus went down the fucking drain, it was wild.

  • Lean into the pain. Oftentimes, procrastination happens because you feel some imaginary pain about the activity that you want to avoid, and so you “procrastinate”--that is, don’t do the work--because you don’t want to feel the pain. But usually, I find that procrastination doesn’t happen this cleanly. It’s not like you feel the pain of the activity when you imagine things, because the activity itself usually isn’t terrible. You are feeling some sort of phantom imaginary pain of the activity while also not thinking about it. It’s like you see the pain in your peripheral vision and you purposefully look away from it. Leaning into the pain is a way around this--when I feel avoidance emotions like this, I will just tell myself: “Yeah, this is going to suck super hard.” And I’m not entirely sure why, but saying this makes me feel better. Try it and let me know how it goes.

  • Let the boredom pass. When working, you will get bored after a while. Maybe this is at five minutes in, or maybe it’s at three hours. Regardless, times like this are where you actually need to lock in and flex that focus muscle. When you get that “I really want to stop” feeling, or the “I’m tired” feeling, stick it out for 10-15 more minutes (tell yourself you can take a break after that if you want). Usually, you’ll find that you hit a second wind, and then you’re back into focus mode. These few reps are the highest leverage times for training your focus. Treating them as a sign to stop is incorrect. That would be like treating muscular fatigue as a sign to stop when lifting weights--like, no! That’s when you have to keep going to build strength in the future! This is the practical way of increasing the amount you work over time.

  • Minimize distractions. Distractions are defined as: anything that is not the work you’re trying to do. The heuristic here is that you should have nothing left to do but the work in front of you. More pithily, “Place yourself in a prison cage.” Here are practical tips for removing distractions:

    • Have Do Not Disturb on at all times. Letting your phone randomly distract you is an insanely stupid idea. This is so basic I’m not going to even explain it.

    • Work in complete silence--don’t listen to music. If you’re doing difficult mental work, music will only slow you down. A million studies confirm this, and it’s experientially obvious if you try working without music for a week. Similarly, noise in general is distracting, even if it’s in the background and relatively benign. Try to block out as much noise as possible. I personally use super thick construction worker earmuffs to block out everything, and work in places that are already silent. I actually used to listen to music in the old days, and then an online study guru told me that I couldn’t really focus while listening to music. I got offended by this, but tried it anyway, and then realized that it’s actually super true and what the hell was I thinking before this!?

    • Work with either: zero people nearby, or accountability police nearby. This has two components: interruptions and visual distractions. The first is that if you work around others, then you are liable to be interrupted by them. The second is that seeing things in your vision is distracting. Try to work with zero people around to avoid both of these. The only reversal of this advice that applies is if these people help really keep you accountable, then it might be a net beneficial thing. But this is a very specialized scenario that doesn’t apply to almost any coworking sessions (e.g. coffee shops, study groups in college, etc.). The accountability police need to also be silent and not interrupt you for this to be net useful.

    • Block every social media app and website--leave no escape. I view myself as a caged animal who will bite and claw and scratch to be let onto the wild terrains of Instagram reels and YouTube philosophy videos. Leave yourself no way of getting onto social media. There are a million plugins for this--just ask Claude about which one to use for your setup. Get one with a password, and then have a friend/partner/opp set the password for you so you don’t know it (or else you will use it). Interestingly, if you go without social media for a few weeks, you will viscerally feel your attention span lengthen, and you’ll stop craving it as much. It’s wild.

  • Sleep well and exercise.

    • Go to bed and wake up around the same time every day. Research shows that sleep consistency beats hours of sleep for most feel-good metrics as long as you’re not getting crazy low amounts of rest.

    • Everyone needs a slightly different amount of sleep--I find that I need around nine hours to feel my best, so I get that nightly. Try and figure out what you need. Warning: there is a subset of people reading this who read the previous sentence as: “I definitely need less than most people because I get that many hours usually (or I have in the past) and I’ve felt fine.” If you thought this, place a massive amount of doubt in your decision to get less than eight hours per night. Statistically, you’re wrong, and I have personally known multiple people who have made serious cases for why they are totally someone who doesn’t need eight hours and then they start getting eight hours and then they feel their IQ rise by fifteen points.

    • Exercise should be a mix of cardiovascular exercise and weight-lifting. Cardio gives you the main brain and mood boost that’s useful for work. Exercise in general structures your day, which is a generally good thing for productivity.

  • Be consistent. In my The Killers are Consistent post, I made the case for why consistency is the main trait all of the greatest scientists, mathematicians, generals, politicians, and entrepreneurs in history had in spades. It’ll come as no surprise that this also applies to being more productive. Try to do something every day to get closer to where you want to go. Even little steps accumulate over time. To intuition pump this, imagine doing a relatively small action--say, reading 20 minutes per day--but every day for a year. That’s 7300 minutes, which at normal reading speeds is around 22-29 books per year. Splitting the difference, 25 books in a year is huge! Imagine how much more knowledgeable you’d be if you knew the contents of 25 more books--gains like that are what consistency unlocks.

More specific things

  • Feel the negative emotion behind procrastination and come up with concrete ways of moving forward. Like I said earlier, usually you only “see” the negative emotion you’re avoiding by procrastinating in your “peripheral vision”. This just means that you refuse to actually feel what you’re avoiding, hence the word “avoiding”. This method just tells you to stop avoiding it. Explicitly name the emotion you’re feeling, e.g. “I am feeling anxious about starting my work because I think it’s going to be really hard because I don’t know how to start this project.” Then, come up with concrete ways of getting into the work, like setting a five-minute timer or leaning into the negative emotion, etc.

  • Make public commitments. I’ve found this to be a really effective technique for doing things consistently. Just tell a community that you care about your social standing in that you’re going to do something. Try to make this a heartfelt thing too, and make your initial commitment message be such that it would be really embarrassing if you fail. Preferably have an external source of verification here, as you might lie to avoid the embarrassment.

  • Threaten donations to anti-charities. If I absolutely had to do something, this is what I’d do. The idea is just that you donate money to an institution you hate if you don’t achieve what you wanted to do, e.g. lose 20lbs or finish a project by its due date, etc. Again, if you can, use an external verifier for whatever your terminal goal is, or else you might cheat “for the greater good”.

  • (Small thing) Use count-down timers instead of count-up timers (i.e. timers instead of a stopwatch), and gradually increase the time until you can work 4+ hours straight. I find that count-down timers make me more focused because I’m on the clock.

  • Use a five-minute timer to break start-up friction. Oftentimes (read: every single time ever) the hardest part of a task is starting it. To get around this, you can use a low-commitment activity like a five-minute timer to “get into” the work without it feeling overwhelming. Once you’re in the work, you almost always just want to continue with it (cf. Neel Nanda).

  • Start almost without thinking. Something that I do that I find works well is starting without thinking in some sense. Like I sit down, then without planning my exact task, “getting ready” to work, or loading my working memory with the task details, I just start the timer and then begin. This is because I find that if I let myself dawdle, I frequently just won’t start. Starting without thinking avoids this failure mode.

Live Your Morals

November 23, 2026 at 12:38:06 PM PST · 6 min read · Substack

Make yourself proud

[This is the 12th of 30 blog posts in my thirty-day blogging series, where I post something every day.]

Today marks the day I become vegan. It’s been a long time coming. Unnecessarily long. Really, it’s just been a can that I’ve kicked down the road by purposefully ignoring the fact that what I was doing was wildly, insanely, despicably immoral. And the funny thing about that is that I’d have been the first one to tell you that what I was doing was wildly, insanely, despicably immoral. But I just. Didn’t. Change.

Similarly, I was convinced of the arguments for AI doom for around a year and a half before seriously pursuing AI safety as my career. Before then, I’d just look at the latest AI news on X, get kind of sad and upset, and then go back to planning how to become the best theoretical physicist possible. I’d then spend exorbitant amounts of time tryharding my way into the best graduate school I could think of. And the thing is: I’ve basically succeeded at this. I got the scholarships in undergrad, published a single-author theoretical physics paper as a junior, took tons of grad classes, graduated in three years with highest honors in my double major in math and physics, and am now in the best theoretical physics master’s program on the planet. And it’s likely that, if I wanted to, I could get into some of the best theoretical physics PhD programs in the world. I did all of this, pursuing the fantasy of being a theoretical physicist, at the exact same time I was espousing the fact that the field would be over in five years, and probably all of humanity with it.

You have to wonder how this can even happen. Like, what? I ate meat multiple times per day, every day, most of it not ethically sourced, while at the same time considering veganism the most obviously correct philosophical position I’ve ever heard? And I spent my days worrying about how to bound the error terms in Appendix A of my physics paper while also thinking that everyone on Earth would probably die in three years from superintelligent AI? What the fuck?

I can tell you right now why I didn’t change my ways earlier: I didn’t feel it in my heart. This is a super cheesy-sounding answer, but it’s true. I only knew these things in my head—I didn’t feel the consequences of my actions. And once I did, I changed my behavior.

I didn’t really feel the fact that hundreds of millions of animals are gruesomely tortured every single day just so humans can enjoy their food somewhat more. I didn’t feel the fact that after they’re born, male chicks are usually placed onto a conveyor belt and then ground up because the egg industry considers them a “waste byproduct”. I also didn’t feel the fact that piglets are oftentimes swung by their hind legs at full force against concrete floors to smash their brains in—this is a common practice in the pork industry called “thumping”. They do it to the runts and other sick or malformed piglets that won’t make enough meat over the course of their lifetime. Thumping usually works in one go. But sometimes not. I didn’t feel the fact that dairy cows have their calves forcibly separated from them shortly after they’re born. If you watch videos of this, both the mother and the calf are screaming out for each other while the calf is dragged away from the mom by one of its hind legs. If the calf is unlucky enough to be a male, then it will shortly be gruesomely killed for veal or painfully raised for 1-2 years and then killed for beef. And if it’s a female? She gets to live the torture-filled life of a dairy cow, being repeatedly artificially inseminated (read: raped) and then separated from her newly born baby, and then of course killed when she’s around five years old, once she’s used up (despite the fact that cows like her would normally live 20 years or longer).

Despite all of this, I just went about eating animal products every single day, multiple times per day, even when there were vegan options available to me. The vegan options just weren’t as tasty and protein-filled as animal-based options, and so I chose the animal option every single fucking time. I did this because my heart wasn’t in it. And as soon as I forced myself to sit with the fact that animal torture was happening at my behest, my heart changed. I just couldn’t fucking live like this anymore. This photo, at the end of Dustin Crummett’s article, Not long now, was the tiny push that sent me over the edge. It made my eyes water, and, honestly, I would have started crying if I hadn’t forced myself not to.

For AI doom, I just didn’t feel the fact that what I was doing in physics was utterly useless and a complete fucking waste of time compared to trying to stop the end of the human race. I wish I could go back in time and shake myself lucid: Like, are you fucking serious? Really? You’re spending hours of your time learning about why ’t Hooft anomalies in 2D systems can be really interestingly interpreted as currents flowing into boundaries? Fuck you, you selfish, myopic prick. It took until I attended the Iliad Intensive in August to change my heart’s disposition toward AI doom. Since then, I’ve been on the path toward AI safety.

I’ve never intimately known someone as selfish as I am. This is, of course, almost necessarily true. But it’s sobering to look at the fact that you are way, way worse than you thought you were—that you don’t live even a fifth of the morals that you espouse to others.

And living your morals is really, really fucking hard. Every single day, when I wake up in Cambridge, all of my peers are concerned with understanding the classes they’re taking, looking smarter than their friends, writing their graduate school essays, and other useless bullshit like that. And I just sit there and steel myself against the urges of statusmaxxing ingrained in me by millions of years of human evolution, further exacerbated by my massive, status-loving ego. Then I proceed to spend the rest of my day fervently working on an issue that most people don’t even give the slightest shit about. And veganism-wise: for the rest of my life, I’m going to enjoy almost all of my meals slightly less than I would if I ate animal products.

But it’s worth it. The satisfaction, the sense of purpose, and the lack of anxiety that I have when I align myself with my morals have always, at the end of the day, been worth it. There simply are higher-order goods in this life. Goods that are worth suffering for. On the other end of the pain of abstinence and prudence are better, stronger feelings of satisfaction, peace, purpose, and duty. Living in congruence with what you think is morally correct pays dividends in your life, and it also actually just feels really good. It rarely feels good in the moment, but it does feel good in the quiet, pensive times when you get to really think about your life: at night before you go to bed, at the end of the year when you look back on your actions, and even immediately after you decide to forgo a morally misaligned action. Spending your time doing things that you think actually make the world a better place feels better than giving in to the expedient vices you currently sedate yourself with.

Live your morals. It’s worth it.

Some feasibility arguments for theoretical AI alignment

November 18, 2026 at 3:24:57 PM PST · 4 min read · Substack

It might actually work

[This is the seventh post in my thirty-day blogging challenge, where I publish something every day for thirty days straight. Tune in for more if you enjoy the post!]

Short post for today, as I’ve been working with my cofounders on building our new theoretical alignment field-building organization, Cambridge Alignment Theory (CAT).

First, what is theoretical AI alignment? Broadly, AI safety1 splits into technical work, policy work, and publicity work. Roughly, technical AI safety work is research done on modern LLMs that tries to make them more aligned with human values. Technical work then splits further into theoretical work and empirical work. Empirical work is research that uses empirical methods to find out how AI systems behave and what interventions exist to make them more aligned. Theoretical work is research that aims to understand AI in mathematical terms to make strong a priori claims about system safety.

I think theoretical alignment research is a low-probability, high-effectiveness bet. That is, I think that it has a low chance of working (~15%), but if it does work, then I think we will get very useful results out of it. By “working”, I roughly mean that someone uses concepts from theoretical alignment research to constrain, predict, or formalize a model’s behavior in a non-trivial way. This is kind of a vague definition, so I will give some examples. For example, if training dynamics found some sort of invariant along gradient flows that constrained a model’s behavior to never say the word “cheese”, I would consider this interesting and useful work. Or if singular learning theory could give some sort of mathematical formalization of what happens during emergent misalignment, I would consider this very interesting. In worlds where results like these are achieved--which by themselves do nothing for alignment--I would expect strong results very useful for alignment shortly afterward.

Now, regardless of the veracity of the claim that theoretical alignment is a low-probability, high-effectiveness bet, it also has a number of other things going for it in general. The first is that it’s an entirely a priori discipline. This means that it’s not constrained by the physical restrictions empirical research is constrained by, like running experiments, training models, etc. The progress can hypothetically move arbitrarily fast, and it isn’t bottlenecked by anything but the intelligence of the model. The second is that theoretical alignment research is an extremely neglected field. There are probably fewer than 100 full-time researchers in this subject, and so adding in an admixture of automated AI labor would either quickly rule out a dead end or rapidly give us interesting results. The third is that mathematics has some notion of “ground truth” in formal proofs. We could theoretically get a verified Lean proof for each output of our model, thus proving that what it did is correct. There are problems with this, mainly in the fact that Lean proofs can be semantically misaligned. This means that what the Lean proof proves is actually not the theorem that we wanted it to prove. There are emerging fixes for this, and I will propose some in my upcoming post on automated alignment research, so I will ignore this for now. One reason why you should think that this might not be a huge issue in the future is that, roughly, reward hacking occurs when reward hacking is easier than actually solving the problem. But the models are already solving Millennium problems. So it follows that if we keep increasing capabilities, Millennium-problem-level research problems will get easier and easier for them, thus decreasing the amount of reward hacking over time. There are also reasonably obvious ways to mitigate semantic misalignment, and people have only been thinking really seriously about this issue for, like, two months. So I trust that we will probably be fine. Scale will solve everything.

NB: If you are interested in (automated) theoretical alignment research, please look into Resolution or MAISI! These institutions are top-tier places for doing theoretical alignment research, and they aim to be hubs for researchers from around the world to do useful work in the area.2 If you’re a math/physics/theoretical CS professor, postdoc, PhD student, or undergrad, I would strongly encourage you to consider looking into theoretical (or empirical) AI safety. And if you’re in Cambridge, UK, then please contact me at wml35@cam.ac.uk to get into our theoretical alignment training and research program.

1

Safety and alignment are interchangeable nouns in this context.

2

There are also other great organizations for theoretical alignment research like ARC, Simplex, or Iliad. (Note that Iliad is mostly a field-building organization as opposed to a research institution.)

Stop Asking for Advice and Go Fucking Do Something

November 17, 2026 at 3:19:50 PM PST · 7 min read · Substack

If you already know the answer to your question, asking it is just procrastination

[This is the sixth post in my thirty-day blogging challenge, where I publish something every day for thirty days straight. Tune in for more if you enjoy the post!]

Warning: this is an Xtreme angerpost note-to-self. You have been warned. While writing this, my mind’s eye was just a Slumdog Millionaire clipshow of all of the times this advice applied to me and the other people I know. If this applies to you too, you’re in good company. There will be lots of cursing in this post, and it’s because I’m really passionate about this topic. I wish I saw something this angry and incisive earlier in my life--maybe I would have changed sooner. I write this in the hope that someone else might be positively affected by the brutality of my writing.

Really smart people have a really stupid failure mode. If the median intelligent person avoided this, then they’d probably be many times more successful. Depending on who you are, this number might be 10x or more. The failure mode is thinking about doing stuff instead of actually doing stuff.

This “thinking about doing stuff” primarily manifests in the forms of planning, advice-taking, and fantasizing.

You plan because you already know what to do, don’t want to do it, but want to feel excited and satisfied about doing something. You spend three hours making a list of fifty different books that you’re “going to read” and get an excited feeling in your stomach about how much you’ll know after you finish them. And then you barely get ten pages into the first book and decide to make a New and Improved fifty-book book list. You write down your goals with pen and paper and tape the pages to your walls, acting like that does literally anything to help you achieve your goals.

You ask for advice (or watch advice online) because you’re searching for an easy way out. You listen to hours of Alex Hormozi and David Goggins on YouTube and get a massive boost of motivation and dopamine, while also conveniently not doing literally anything to achieve your goals. It’s socially acceptable to seek “guidance” when trying to pursue a goal, so it feels like you’re actually making progress towards something real when you take advice. You’re not.

You fantasize because you want the achievement of the end goal without the effort that it requires. You pace around your room at 11pm while listening to Brazilian phonk, rap music, or Hans Zimmer in your AirPods while pretending you’re in a TikTok edit just to feel important. You think about what you’d say on a podcast after you achieve your goals because you want the feeling of having actually done something without having to do anything.

The fact of the matter is that doing anything but the actual thing is a waste of time. And I’m not going to add the qualifier “almost always” in front of “a waste of time” even though it’s actually correct. This is because the procrastinator weasel-mind inside of you will read “almost always” and milk it to mean something closer to 80% of the time. So no. Just do the thing. Anything else is a waste of time.

There just comes a point where you just have to actually fucking do the thing. The hard thing that actually gets you where you want to go. The super obvious thing that you already know you have to do. If you just replaced all of your mental masturbation about the thing with taking action on the thing itself, you’d probably be 5x more successful in every area of life than you are now. Some examples to see what I mean:

  • Let’s say you want to lose weight. Instead of spending four hours investigating 100 different exercise programs and 15 different diets, just fucking go on a run right then and there and then eat less at your next meal. Figure out your master plan after the action has been taken. Because taking action makes you realize that your health-coded Dungeons and Dragons world-building was a complete fucking waste of time, and that--shockingly--you should actually just consistently do all of the super obvious things that you already know you should do. And maybe you need to read for an hour on what lifting is or have a coach show you how to do it. Whatever. Get the knowledge then fucking go to work. There is no getting around it. You just have to put in the effort and it’s going to be difficult. Get over it. Avoiding it by planning and mythologizing isn’t going to fucking do anything.

  • Let’s say you want to get better grades. You could watch 100 hours of study content on YouTube, or you could just fucking study for two hours, right then and there. And then maybe you realize that, oh, you suck at studying because you have no idea how to do it. So then you watch one video on it and then study again using the techniques it talked about. And then you find out that, oh, you still suck at studying because you’re really distracted. So then you get rid of all social media, stop listening to music when you study, block all notifications, and study alone in a room with nothing on the walls. And wow, you study so much better now because you’re so much more focused! Gee whiz, who could have thought up such a miraculous solution to your problem? Real talk: does it take a fucking genius to figure out how to increase your focus? Really? How about you do all of the insanely obvious things that any ten-year-old could figure out and then just fucking do them? “Oh but studying without music is boring”, “Oh but you don’t understand I have ADHD so I can’t focus”, “Oh but this won’t work for me because I’m a visual learner”. Fuck you. My ears translate all of these statements as “I’m a punk bitch and I will be nothing in life”. Enjoy your nothingness in life, you punk bitch. Figure it the fuck out or resign yourself to being a spineless loser.

  • Let’s say you want a romantic partner. You could just bitch and whine about how “cooked” the dating market is, or write entire essays in your head about why you in particular are actually just undateable or whatever else your loser mind can come up with. And you do this while having literally tried nothing. You make sweeping statements about yourself and others, and when I query you even the tiniest bit about your beliefs, I find out that you are literally just making shit up. Instead of it being “cooked” and you being “undateable”, you actually just haven’t made eye contact with a woman since January and have exclusively conversed with the same five male math and physics friends that you’ve known for multiple years. What if, instead of waxing poetic about how Clavicular was right and the blackpill is true and blah blah blah whatever, you just found all of the places near you that allowed you to have interpersonal communication with people of your desired gender, and then talked to all of them? And then asked the ones you were interested in for their phone numbers? And then tried to go on dates with them? Or you could just download a dating app and swipe until you find success. “But what if no one wants to date me? 🥺,” says the loser chud who will always find flaws in any solution given to them. Gee, I don’t know loser chud, what should you do? How about fucking figure out why you’re not dateable and then do all of the super obvious things that you can think of to fix it! (E.g., if you’re fat eat less and move more, if you’re skinny eat more and hit the gym, if you’re awkward talk to more people and see if you can get better each time, if you have bad hygiene just fucking fix it, etc.)

At some point you have to stop figuring out the optimal way to do things and just actually do them. Because you know what’s not optimal? Not doing anything at all. And that’s what you’ll end up doing if you let your anxious optimization-focused intellect take the wheel without biasing toward action.

Take this warning to heart. Stop asking for advice, planning, and fantasizing about the work and just do it. If you decide not to listen to this, you will achieve maybe 1/10th of your potential, if you’re lucky. I absolutely promise you that you will be beaten by so many people so much less talented than you, to the point of even your worst enemy cringing a little bit. It’s up to you to choose which life you want to live.

Avoid the Sin of Sloth

November 16, 2026 at 3:03:30 PM PST · 6 min read · Substack

Jesus was a really cool guy with lots of great ideas

[This is the fifth post in my thirty-day blogging challenge, where I publish something every day for thirty days straight. Tune in for more if you enjoy the post!]

The Biblical corpus is something I’ve gradually come to see as probably the greatest source of wisdom in human history. In some sense, the teachings of Jesus seem to have boiled down the key principles of behavior that everyone ought to abide by. And like many others that periodically treat themselves to a relaxing scroll on Substack, I oftentimes come across new and interesting philosophical principles, rationality techniques, or self-help ideas. I will then frequently have this funny experience where I take three licks of the Tootsie Pop that is the philosophical idea I’m pondering, and then I’ll find out that it’s basically just a corollary of something Jesus said 2000 years ago. And for the record, I’m an agnostic—I’m under no obligation to bind my intellect to the teachings of the very interesting Jewish rabbi that lived in Judea around 0BC. But I still end up finding that oftentimes the best way to phrase things is by using fantastical, Jordan Peterson-like scriptural language.

There are two closely related, but distinct, ideas that I was thinking about today, and after contemplating them for a bit, I found that they are best characterized through the idea of sloth1. Aquinas defines sloth as “an oppressive sorrow, which, to wit, so weighs upon man’s mind, that he wants to do nothing.” This is the feeling you get after a good ol’ Instagram reels scrollfest. Or after you eat tons of really bad food and get the strong sense that you will probably have to roll, as opposed to walk, to your next destination.

And while gooning is super fun and all the kids do it nowadays, I think it’s useful to take a step back and consider the following idea. I truly believe that if you excised, say, 1000 people from history, humanity would be set back multiple centuries. And if you were to ask me what I really think, I would say that this number is probably closer to 1000 years or more. Imagine what the world would be like if Elon Musk, Albert Einstein, Alexander Fleming, Jesus, George Washington, and Napoleon just never existed. While I can’t make any precise statements here, I get the very strong general feeling that things would be way worse. The fact that these people existed and did what they did has benefited billions of people throughout history, and if AI doesn’t kill everyone, will benefit quadrillions more. This kind of EV calculation is the type of thing that vegan EA longtermists go crazy for. But even if you disagree with the logical consequences of extrapolating this position (e.g. that you are morally worse than ten billion Hitlers if you don’t optimize every second of your day to benefit the lives of the quintrillion future people living near Alpha Centauri), it still seems correct that you owe the future of humanity your best effort at making things go well. I phrase this as: you are morally compelled to try your hardest.

It’s an admirable thing to see someone try their best, regardless of what that is. Some people’s best is literally going to be 1,000,000x more productive than someone else’s best—but as long as you are trying your best, this is all anyone can ask for. Putting your finest effort into developing yourself and the world at large is, in some sense, all we really can do on this Earth.

And this brings me to the next idea I was thinking about. It’s a simple one, but I think it’s a useful clarification to the sigma grindset Try Harder mentality it might seem like I was just talking about above. It’s the idea that you shouldn’t waste time. I’m going to put this in a block quote to make it more dramatic:

Don’t. Waste. Time.

Not wasting time is something that your grandmother as well as the random homeless guy on the street could tell you to do. So why am I block-quoting this in a somewhat philosophical-sounding Substack post? It’s because the obvious advice is the advice that actually works—that’s why everyone comes up with it. Humans repeatedly fall prey to the same errors in their reasoning and actions, and since we all roughly try not to keep hitting ourselves in the face, we tend to come to a lot of the same conclusions. But since everyone has heard the idea of not wasting time more frequently than they’ve heard to brush their teeth, the recommendation becomes tired and ineffectual.

But everyone knows that this advice, if followed, would probably make the life they’re living at least 2x better. And we still don’t follow it for some reason. I have some concrete solutions here, and I’ve talked about a few of them in my other posts (here, and here). But the reason I brought this up is because that, while I believe that you are morally compelled to try your best, I think that can give the wrong image. I think a lot of people imagine that when I say, “try your best”, I really mean that you should be spending 16hrs/day in the office while strategically missing all of your daughter’s ballet recitals. But this isn’t quite right. What’s more accurate is that I think you shouldn’t waste your time. And the funny thing about this is that you get to decide what constitutes a waste of time! You’re not always going to be right, and you’ll err less and less as time goes on, but you are the judge, jury, and executioner of your own life. So if you think that cultivating quality relationships with your romantic partner and children is a good use of your time, then actually do that! And if you think that brutally debate-mogging all of your haters on X is a waste of time, then don’t do that!2 You get to play by the rules of the game you set up, and by your own standards, you are probably wasting at least four hours per day, if not more. The cardinal sin of sloth comes for us all—it’s up to us to go to war with it every single day for the only truly non-renewable resource we have: time.

And so I hope that this Substack post, which is really just a note to self, gently reminds you that you might only have a few years left on Earth, and that you should vicariously live your moral values every single second of every single day that you have left.

More generally, this is a thoroughly Christian idea, which is the main set of ideas this post is engaging with.

The Killers are Consistent

November 15, 2026 at 2:42:57 PM PST · 6 min read · Substack

Tony Stark is a fictional character—if you try to act like him, you’re an idiot

[This is the fourth post in my thirty-day writing challenge, where I write one blog post every day for thirty days. Tune in for more if you enjoy the post!]

Charlie Munger—one of the greatest investors of all-time—was famous for a way of thinking called inversion. The idea is that, instead of asking how to succeed at something, ask what are all of the ways that you could fail at something, and then avoid all of those. The efficacy of this technique is based in the fact that it’s easier to imagine ways you can fail as opposed to ways you can succeed.

Charlie applied this frame to success in general, trying to find all of the ways someone could act to be unsuccessful. And he was gracious enough to share this advice with the Harvard-Westlake class of 1986. His first original piece of advice in the speech was the easiest and most surefire way he saw to live a life of utter misery and complete failure:

“First, be unreliable. Do not faithfully do what you have engaged to do. If you will only master this one habit you will more than counterbalance the combined effect of all your virtues, howsoever great. If you like being distrusted and excluded from the best human contribution and company, this prescription is for you. Master this one habit and you can always play the role of the hare in the fable, except that instead of being outrun by one fine turtle you will be outrun by hordes and hordes of mediocre turtles and even by some mediocre turtles on crutches.

I must warn you that if you don’t follow my first prescription, it may be hard to end up miserable, even if you start disadvantaged. I had a roommate in college who was and is severely dyslexic. But he is perhaps the most reliable man I have ever known. He has had a wonderful life so far, with an outstanding wife and children and as chief executive of a multibillion-dollar corporation.

If you want to avoid a conventional, main-culture, establishment result of this kind, you simply can’t count on your other handicaps to hold you back if you persist in being reliable.”

Unreliability here has two aspects: trust with others and consistency of behavior. The latter trait is something that has hit me over the head over and over again when studying the greats of history—they are astonishingly consistent. Here are some examples to see what I mean:

  • Alexander Grothendieck, the greatest mathematician of the 20th century, worked 12hrs/day, 7days/week, for ten years straight. He would write mathematics through the night, give his hand-written notes to Jean Dieudonné in the morning, sleep for a few hours, and then would lecture in seminar during the day. This gave rise to a reformulation and extension of algebraic geometry that permanently changed the face of mathematics.

  • Henri Poincaré, one of the greatest mathematicians of all time and the “Last Universalist” in mathematical history, worked from 10am-12pm and 5-7pm every day, probably for decades.

  • Winston Churchill, the leader of Britain in WW2 and winner of the Nobel Prize for Literature, lived the same day for decades, regardless of whether there was a war ravaging all of Europe. He rose around 8am and worked from his bed until 11am, where he would read various papers and dictate writings to secretaries. He then bathed and had lunch, after which he would work a bit more, and then have a nap and then eat dinner. Then from around 8pm-2am, he would work more. Interestingly, he kept this exact schedule—including his evening nap—even during the Blitz!

  • Pablo Picasso, the most prolific artist of the 20th century, would sleep in late, have lunch, and then paint from 2pm until late into the night. He did this every day for 75 years, and produced around 50,000 works.

  • Benjamin Franklin, scientist, writer, diplomat, and Founding Father, followed a strict daily schedule, working around eight hours per day. He rose at 5am and then planned and studied until around 8am. He then worked from 8am-12pm, and then read and ate from 12-2pm. He worked again from 2-6pm, and then spent 6-10pm on dinner, music, and conversation.

  • Immanuel Kant, arguably the most important philosopher of the modern era, lived basically the same exact day over and over again in his town of Königsberg. He would rise at 5am, lecture and write until around 1pm, and then have his single meal of the day: a long lunch with a rotating group of his friends. He then walked at the exact same hour every day, then he read, and then he slept at 10pm. This is the routine that produced, for example, the Critique of Pure Reason.

  • John D. Rockefeller, arguably the most successful businessman of all time, followed the same daily routine “down to the second” (cf. Ron Chernow, his biographer). One of Rockefeller’s friends called it “unbroken, mathematically perfect... uncanny.” During his tenure as the CEO of Standard Oil (which is the reason anti-trust laws exist today), Rockefeller arrived at 9:15am sharp every morning. He was then shaved by a barber, worked, had crackers and milk mid-morning, worked more, and then had lunch with his executives at noon. He then took a nap after lunch. Rockefeller then usually worked from home until nighttime, having run a telegraph wire from the office to his house.

  • Charles Dickens, the most popular novelist of the Victorian era and one of the most famous writers of all time, arose around 7am every day and then worked in “complete silence” from 9am-2pm. He then walked for three hours, had dinner at 6pm, and then spent the evening with his family and friends. He followed this schedule for roughly 34 years straight.

I think there is oftentimes this idea of a “mad genius”, or in business, the “Tony Stark”-type character who’s an inconsistent but amazing, disorganized giga-IQ individual that somehow gets tons of stuff done while also being wildly variable day-to-day. But this archetype is actually vanishingly rare if you peruse the history books. It does exist—Isaac Newton, for example, worked around 16-18 hours per day during his writing of the Principia, barely eating or sleeping to have more time to work on his theories of physics and mathematics. Newton would then work much more tamely in other periods of his life. But there are downsides to having this sort of maniacal obsession with work—people like this, even the great ones, can seriously suffer in their mental health, possibly due to their working habits. Newton, for example, had a nervous breakdown in 1693 after working this way during the years of 1684-1687. Grothendieck also became a reclusive schizophrenic after leaving mathematics in the 1970’s.

So what to take away from this? My main observation here is that the killers are consistent. History has empirically shown us that the most productive and intelligent people of all time are astonishingly consistent. They stick to roughly—or exactly—the same schedule every day for years or decades at a time. These people are way smarter than you and put in way more hours than you. It’s a pure ego play to assume that you can do anything similar to what they did without their level of consistency over time. Consistency is the most difficult of traits to cultivate, but it’s one that—if mastered—historically produces outsized results for the person serious enough to cultivate it.

Don’t Use “Neglected” as a Synonym for “Useless”

November 14, 2026 at 2:28:40 PM PST · 5 min read · Substack

Neglectedness is an EV multiplier, not a dispositive reason

[This is the third post in my thirty-day writing challenge where I publish one blog post daily. Tune in for more if you enjoy the post!]

There is a funny word that is almost exclusive to the vernacular of the EA community, and this word bestows downright magical powers on its users.

The word I’m talking about is neglected.

I view this word as a sort of “Get Out of Jail Free” card for doing the most useless things you’ve ever heard of. Something that will happen to you if you go to parties in SF is that people will tell you what they do, and their statements will almost always be very interesting. And sometimes, you will hear something like this:


“I run a startup making fur-lined boots for honey bees. As it turns out, honey bees get really cold feet in the winter, and this diminishes their well-being by around 20%. Since there are so many honey bees, and because there are tons of butterfly effects from honey bees being happier, this would produce a sizable—but not enormous—positive good for the world. And it’s a really neglected problem, so I think it’s a pretty good use of my time.”1

You will then smile, nod, and then excuse yourself to eat some Oreos or one of the other vegan snacks they have at the party. That is, unless you’re really pushy, in which case you will ask them tons of questions about why they are doing what they’re doing. Because, like, come on. Really? That’s what you’re doing? Out of all of the things on the planet that you could spend your time on, you chose to make fur-lined boots for honey bees?

Normally I’d try to steelman this point of view. But I’ve pushed a lot of people who say things like this, and I’ve literally never heard a single good argument from any of them. It always just bottoms out in “it seems like a good idea”, or “it’s pretty neglected...”. Even if you take this person’s speculative numbers at face value, they are doing—at best—a pretty modest good. And this just pales in comparison to other things they could be doing, regardless of how neglected this current issue is. There simply isn’t an argument here. It’s just a reasoning error that the EA community frequently doesn’t punish because the person making the case used the fancy in-group word “neglected”.

To see why the case of our candidate Uggs for Bees™ CEO fails, I think it’d be useful to reflect on why neglectedness is a useful metric to judge candidate careers by. The reason neglectedness is a useful metric is simply that it raises the EV of the career in question. That’s it. Specifically, let’s say you agree that you should aim your career towards doing good. Make it bigger and your expected impact increases, make it smaller and it decreases. In words, neglectedness matters because it raises the expected impact of you in particular working on a given field.

Practically, this occurs because the fruit in a field start out low-hanging, and then become progressively harder to pick as you climb up the tree of popularity. So, the expected return of an additional person working in the field is very high. For example, it’s probably more useful to be the first wild-insect-suffering reducer than the hundredth chicken-suffering reducer. Wild insect suffering is a super big deal, and there is basically no one working on it. It’s also really tractable—if you lobbied for the use of certain pesticides over others then you’d help literally billions (and possibly trillions) of insects live much better lives. Chicken suffering at the hands of factory farms is also a super big deal, but the marginal impact of another person working on it probably isn’t as high as working on wild insect suffering. So, ceteris paribus, it follows that you should work on wild insect suffering. Or, if you don’t like that example, then it’s probably more useful to start an AI safety compute cluster than to be the hundredth person on a frontier lab’s safety team. A field being neglected is connotatively synonymous with the field being easy to positively impact. One more person working in a neglected field might be worth ten people working in a non-neglected field.

But notice that all we’re doing here is an EV calculation. So neglectedness by itself says literally nothing about whether or not something is worth doing—you must consider the concomitant reasons of scale and tractability when considering what to do. For example, a super neglected question is whether or not energy drinks are actually superintelligent morally conscious agents. This would be a really big deal if true, as superintelligences probably feel suffering to a higher degree than humans do, and there are lots of energy drinks. And it’s also a completely neglected field! The horror! But obviously you should not work on this. The EV of this field is vanishingly small, and so its degree of neglect doesn’t matter.2

So now we can come full circle and see why our Uggs for Bees™ start-up was doomed from the beginning—its EV was simply much lower than tons of other alternatives.

In summary, when doing EV calculations about whether or not your career does good, it’s not enough to just consider how neglected a field is—you must consider its overall impact. You may find that it’s not as high as a number of other options, in which case you should strongly consider doing one of those other options. Your time on Earth is short—do something useful with it.

Realize When You’re Stupid

November 13, 2026 at 2:33:56 PM PST · 12 min read · Substack

Strap yourself to the mast to save yourself from the Sirens

[This post is the second post in my thirty day writing challenge. Tune in for more if you enjoy it!]

If you reliably knew that you would make a bad decision during a certain time, would you let yourself make decisions during that time? Probably not, right? So why do you let yourself grocery shop when hungry, speak negatively when you have low blood sugar, and make key decisions when anxious?

A core epistemic skill that I’ve tried to cultivate over the years is the idea of knowing when I’m stupid, and not letting myself make decisions during these times. Like all core epistemic skills, I’ve cultivated this in response to getting repeatedly smacked across the face over and over again.

I.

Cindy is an anxious woman. Her resting heart rate is a bit high, she gets wigged out easily, and she tends to run an endless stream of disaster loops through her head at all hours of the day. Like all anxious people, her anxiety rises and falls. Maybe it rises when her blood sugar is low, when she’s under-slept, or when other things in her life are particularly stressful. Probably all three.

She, like all people, has a number of decisions to make throughout the day. Some of these decisions are big and some of them are small. Cindy reliably finds that when she’s flooded with anxiety, she repeatedly runs into certain failure modes. For example, maybe she makes elaborate plans to fix every single problem in her entire life, half-commits to it for, like, one day, and then decides that: no, what was she thinking? That plan is terrible! It’s time for another ground-breaking, earth-shaking plan that will fix every single problem in her entire life. She then half-commits to this plan, and then one day later comes to a terrible realization....

Cindy notices that this kind of planning loop stops her from making any actual progress. It’s like she’s in a car and wants to go in a certain direction, but instead she goes into a hard right turn, and then a hard left turn, and then a hard right turn, and then a hard... you get the idea. If Cindy just stuck with a plan, even if it was kind of mediocre, then she would be able to make some sort of progress over time. It’s similar to how a random walk has an expected displacement of zero, but a directed random walk has an expected displacement that grows like n, where n is the number of steps.

Since Cindy notices that she reliably gets the extremely strong urge to stop literally everything she’s doing and make a Plan to Rule All Plans when she feels very anxious, it would be a good idea for Cindy to ban herself from planning when she feels this way—to make a deontological bar1 against planning when anxious. This would likely mitigate the failure mode, as Cindy only really feels the urge to stop, drop, and re-plan when she’s very anxious, and really not otherwise.

Another way of saying this is that Cindy realized that she’s invariably stupid in this particular way when she’s super anxious, and so she bans herself from making decisions during this time.

II.

Tom, like many members of technical staff at frontier labs, is highly stressed when he comes home from work. Tom pretty frequently takes this out on either himself or his partner. He takes it out on himself by binge eating food, and takes it out on his partner by (somewhat unintentionally) being cold, snippy, and short with her.

In the moment, this of course feels justified on his end. Those donuts aren’t going to eat themselves. Tom is, after all, a researcher on the pre-training team, and so he uses his brain all day; chess players have to consume lots of calories during chess tournaments to fuel their mental energy expenditure—the same probably applies to pretraining research, right? This thought, and many others, is one of the concomitant lines of justification that Tom’s +3SD-IQ brain cooks up when he’s hungry and wants to post hoc justify his strong urge to eat terrible food—food that he unfailingly regrets eating afterward.

Similarly, in the moment, it feels correct to be rude to his partner. She asked him a dumb question, and he gave an answer that is already more graceful than should be expected of him. It’s not his fault that she can’t act like the other pretraining researchers and “just get it” the first time. But yet, after Tom eats his nightly DoorDashed Mexican food, he can’t help but feel bad for how he acted when he walked through the door. She’s not a frontier AI researcher—of course she doesn’t know the persnickety details of what he’s doing during the day. Tom ends up apologizing whenever this happens, and resolves for it to not happen again. But yet, it reliably does happen when he comes home.

It would be useful here for Tom to recognize when he’s stupid, and place a deontological bar against taking certain actions during this time. Tom reliably eats poor-quality food and is rude to his partner when he gets home. So Tom decides to place the rule that he (a) can’t eat any junk food until after dinner, and (b) has to be Jesus-levels of kind to his partner before eating dinner. This is because through empirical experience, if Tom leaves the decision to the moment when he’s stupid, he’ll make the wrong one. So he takes away the decision-making power from himself until he’s out of his stupidity.

III.

Claire is a normal human being with a strong imagination. Maybe she also leans anxious, but it’s not really noticeable during the day. But Claire has the funny recurring experience of waking up in the middle of the night feeling super anxious, or feeling like something could go horribly wrong at any moment. This often causes her serious fear and anxiety, and sometimes it causes her to take certain actions, like check her phone, turn the lights on to scan the room, and carefully craft a salt ring around her bed to prevent any Catholic demons from getting her. Whenever she finally gets back to sleep and wakes up the next morning, she thinks back to how she acted during the night and almost morbidly laughs at how stupid her actions were. She thinks of the same things that brought her anxiety at 2am, and is completely unaffected by them.

It would serve Claire well to realize when she’s stupid, and bar herself from taking any remedial actions during this time. She has empirically found that she makes downright irrational decisions during the short time period after waking up, and so she bars herself from doing anything but ignoring her scary or anxious thoughts and going back to bed. She accepts the fact that she might come across a false negative—maybe her anxious thoughts about whether or not her parents were ok combined with her 0.7% credence that ESP is real were really telling her something that one time. But probably not.

So, Claire places a deontological bar on making any decisions during this time—she pre-makes the decision when she’s smart for the times where she’s stupid.

IV.

(non-fictional example)

Bryan Johnson—the most measured man on the planet—used to consistently binge-eat every night after getting back from building Braintree Venmo. Bryan was obviously a hyper-competent individual: extremely hard-working and intelligent, with focus so sharp you could cut diamonds with it. But during the hours of 5-10pm, “Evening Bryan” took over. Evening Bryan was stressed from the day, didn’t get great sleep the night before, had multiple young kids, and also had extreme depression. (Well, Work Bryan also had all of these—but Work Bryan was occupied with work, so he didn’t really have the chance to do something like this.) Evening Bryan was a caged animal, and an entire pan of Betty Crocker Chocolate Fudge brownies was his freedom. Bryan would always binge eat tremendous amounts of food regardless of what the better-meaning Bryans wanted him to do.

So Bryan placed a deontological bar against eating during the hours of 5-10pm. Evening Bryan would valiantly protest against this dictatorial ordinance, but it worked. He stopped binge-eating, and Bryan actually credits this as one of the first steps he took toward his current Blueprint lifestyle.

V.

Around 3200 years ago, when Odysseus crossed the Tyrrhenian Sea off the coast of Southern Italy, he knew that he had to sail past the Sirens that lived on the rocks in those waters. The Sirens were known for their song—a song that compelled all men who listened to it to claw their way off of the boat and dive into the water, swimming directly into the arms of the topless bird women singing that wondrous melody. These men were then inevitably killed by these topless bird women singing that wondrous melody. But the song sure was pretty.

Knowing this devilish information, Circe instructed Odysseus to stuff his sailors’ ears with wax, thus blocking the Sirens’ song from piercing their minds.

But Odysseus wanted more.

Odysseus wanted to hear the song himself. But he knew that upon hearing their sublime song, he would surely jump to his death. So Odysseus decided to bar himself from making a decision at this time by lashing himself to the mast of his black Achaean ship. In other words, Odysseus realized when he was going to be stupid, and then placed a deontological bar on making decisions during that time.

VI.

Will Lancer2 is highly disagreeable, confident, and has strong opinions. He also considers himself pretty fair, in the sense that he will try his hardest to steelman his opponent. But even with this internal evaluation of fairness, he reliably finds that when he encounters a new view, he will frequently form very strong opinions about it extremely quickly. These opinions will usually be to the tune of “This view is completely and utterly insane, and it is mind-blowing to me that anyone rational holds it”. He will then propagate his views in a normal manner by telling others about them in-person and online.

But Will reliably finds that, after further inspection of these positions, they were actually stronger than he gave them credit for. Maybe they aren’t something he fully agrees with, but there are serious arguments for the position. For example, Will could imagine that if he had slightly different intuitive credences on a few key premises weighing on the issue, then he would believe the viewpoint he once thought was ludicrous.

So it would probably be useful for Will to bar himself from speaking harsh words about something unless he has rigorously investigated the opposing viewpoint, say, to the point of being able to write an essay on why it’s correct. Will realizes that he’s frequently wrong and stupid when the combination of relative ignorance + extreme dismissiveness arises in response to an idea, and so he shouldn’t speak with confidence about an issue before thoroughly steelmanning3 the opposing side.4

We’ve seen a lot of instances where someone realizes that they predictably make bad decisions when they’re in a certain headspace, and so this person bars themselves from taking certain actions during this time. This bar can be on specific things, like eating food, or it can be a universal bar on decision-making in general, with a pre-filled decision made ahead of time (like Claire with her post-wake anxiety and fear).

In Googling around for this post, I found that this practice actually already has a neat name: a Ulysses contract. According to Wikipedia, a Ulysses contract is a “freely made decision that is designed and intended to bind oneself in the future.” Interestingly, its name is derived from Example V above—of Odysseus (Ulysses) strapping himself to the mast, thus making a binding choice to restrict himself in the future.

The key difference between a Ulysses contract and a good ol’ fashioned resolution is the strictness of it. You should think of your future self as a caged animal that will fight with its dying breath to get what it wants in the moment—the only way to fight a creature that has just as much intelligence as you, that’s just as good at arguing as you are, and that knows all of the rationality tricks you do is to just not fight it in the first place. Get rid of your ability to make a decision in the moment rather than imploring yourself to situationally make the correct one.

If you find yourself reliably making the same bad decisions at a certain time or while you’re in a certain headspace, consider making a Ulysses contract with yourself to avoid the temptation of the Sirens.

You Can Get Over Your Three-Letter Disorder

November 12, 2026 at 11:14:09 AM PST · 19 min read · Substack

Can you people do ANYTHING?!?

[This post is the first post of my thirty-day writing project, where I write one blog post per day for thirty days straight. Tune in for more!]

I, for most of my life, could have been easily identified as someone with strong ADHD, OCD, and anxiety symptoms. Now I don’t really have these symptoms. What changed, and why do I think most people can do something like this?

Disclaimer: this is an opinion post done by someone without a shred of laboratory evidence for their thesis. Take it as such. I’m going to use words in this post like “definitely” and “totally possible”—this reflects what I really think (i.e., what I’d bet money on), and it’s supported by my personal experience. The plural of personal anecdote is empirical, right? Anyway.... If you disagree with me, then fine. I don’t care. You’re right. You win. I don’t know you. I’m unaware that you’re actually the one person this advice doesn’t apply to. Awesome. You can’t get over it. You’re doomed forever. Stop reading now and save your time.

Introduction

The short answer on what changed from my childhood: I systematically changed my behavior to not exhibit any more symptoms of these disorders. To show you that this answer isn’t as trite as it seems, I’d like to take a step back and show you how I think about “three-letter disorders” (OCD, ADD, ADHD, GAD, MDD, etc.) in general. I think this is most easily introduced via an example.

Let’s say there is a kid named Johnny in your class in school, and Johnny is hyperactive, impulsive, forgetful, and a procrastinator. You end up getting tired of being around Johnny and his loser self, and so you ask him:

“Johnny, why are you so damn lazy? You are forgetful, impulsive, a procrastinator, and you distract everyone around you too! What the hell is wrong with you?”

Johnny replies,

“Well, I have ADHD! It’s not my fault that I do all of these things. My ADHD makes me hyperactive, impulsive, forgetful, and a procrastinator.”

Your eyebrow raises and you say,

“Seems suspicious Johnny—how do you even know that you have ADHD in the first place?”

With an eye roll, Johnny replies

“Ugh, I obviously have ADHD because I’m hyperactive, impulsive, forgetful, and a procrastinator!”

Wait—does Johnny have ADHD because he does all of those things, or does he do all of those things because he has ADHD? To answer this question, it might be useful to zoom out and see how psychological disorders are thought about in practice.

A rough picture of disorder classification and diagnosis

Let’s say you have some collection of traits, like forgetfulness, attention span, impulsivity, extraversion, agreeableness, etc. These traits are assumed to roughly be normally distributed—that is, it’s expected that most people have roughly average amounts of any given trait. As it turns out, some of these traits covary. Doing empirical tests tells you that for certain people, if you find out that they have a high degree of forgetfulness, they actually also have a higher likelihood of having a hard time regulating their attention span. You also find that they are unusually impulsive and tend to wildly procrastinate. You find that there are actually big clumps of traits—characterized entirely by behaviors—that tend to strongly covary in certain people, and in fact, the traits that are present are present to high amounts. Amounts that cause real negative interference in their life.

The most parsimonious explanation of this data is that there is actually one factor causing all of these different traits—let’s call this factor A for now. Some other names I considered were D and H, and actually D again. But anyway. If you find out that someone has property A, then you can reliably predict that they will exhibit a selection of traits, demonstrating each one of which to a level that negatively interferes with their life.1

This is roughly how psychological disorders are discovered and subsequently diagnosed.2 Our property A can be something like OCD, ADHD, GAD, MDD, etc. (This psychometrics-style analysis is also the cause of the best personality and IQ classifications.) These properties are abstract labels meant to classify the fact that the person with the label exhibits a multitude of correlated traits that negatively impact their lives due to their severity. These traits are assumed to have a root cause largely in a single factor, like weird dopamine uptake in the brain, or an overactive amygdala.

Inferences

There are two pertinent things to point out here. The first is that the diagnosis of a disorder is entirely a behavioral analysis. (Note that I’m counting thought patterns as behaviors—they are simply behaviors of the mind.) Disorders are diagnosed by what you do. If you didn’t do any of those things, then you would be indistinguishable from someone without the disorder, short of a brain scan. The second thing to point out is that a disorder doesn’t make you do anything. It just biases you towards certain behaviors and not others. When you procrastinate on your homework, it’s not because a physical ADHD demon was sitting next to you, firmly gripping your right thumb, and swiping up on Instagram reels for three hours straight—it was because you chose to doomscroll!

What’s the punchline of this? It’s that disorders are characterized by behavior, and you control your behavior, and thus you can control whether or not you “have” a disorder. If I’m focused, don’t forget things, am not impulsive, and don’t procrastinate, then I consider the fact that you ascribe the 1st, 4th, 8th, and 4th letter of the alphabet to my behavior a causally effete description. If a label can’t predict what I do, then it’s irrelevant.

*You control whether or not you have a disorder. Period.*

There are clear objections to what I’m writing here. I’m not going to exhaustively respond to them, at least in part because some of them are actually right. I believe that a small fraction of people can’t change their behavior to the point of their symptoms not dramatically impacting their life. I’m lucky that I got the level of disorders that only caused one super serious breakdown and a few failed classes instead of, like, a lifetime of misery and agoraphobia. If you are one of the unlucky few with serious mental conditions, you have my overwhelming empathy.

However, if you’re reading this, that’s simply not you. There is an overwhelming chance you are not one of these select few individuals that God chose to YOLO the serotonin and dopamine receptors of. And if you think you are, you’re probably wrong.3 Stop coping with your own failures, and take accountability for your actions. You can change the fact that you have a three-letter disorder. It just takes effort and the right way of going about things.

Steelman of the opposing viewpoint and objection handling

I think the strongest steelman of the opposing viewpoint is something like this:

“This post is wrong on a few levels. First, a psychological diagnosis is far more complicated than you let on—it takes a trained professional multiple sessions to be able to reliably discern whether or not someone has a disorder, and they have to train for, like, a decade to be able to do it accurately. Second, rejecting a label is just cope. You just don’t like the fact that you have [X] disorder, so you say that it’s all a behavioral thing and blah blah causally effete blah blah. Just because you refuse to ascribe a label to yourself obviously doesn’t mean that it doesn’t still affect your life. Third, by making this into a heroic sigma-grindset willpower thing, you’re foregoing some of the best actual treatments for your disorder. Psychologists aren’t idiots—if telling someone to ‘just change your behavior, bro’ worked, they would have done it by now. The things that actually work are medication and therapy, which show really good results in most cases. By placing the burden entirely on yourself, you ignore the most effective ways to get treatment.

Also, was your armchair nice and comfy when you wrote this post? I hope it wasn’t. You are really showcasing your ignorance about (a) psychology in general and (b) the lived experience of people with these disorders by writing this piece. Also, if you take what you said to its natural conclusion, then it would follow that psychologists shouldn’t study people with these disorders to try and make their lives better—y’know, since it’s all a behavior thing, right? All of the people helped by CBT, ERP, SSRIs, antipsychotic medication. Yeah, those people should have just tried harder instead. Lmao. Do better man.”

Whew. That was a scathing takedown of my position. Because of this, I’d like to respond with some clarification of what I actually think.

First, the psychological diagnosis picture I painted was indeed a picture, and not a photorealistic one at that. It’s more complicated than that. But really, the essential characteristics are there. The actual way psychological disorders are characterized in the DSM is by expert consensus and clinical observation—not the factor analysis picture I was painting earlier. Diagnostically, it takes a psychologist or psychiatrist interviewing you, along with first-order evidence from your childhood, to come to an accurate diagnosis of a disorder. The point I really wanted to make with the rough scene I depicted was that a “diagnosis” isn’t a psychologist reading the tea leaves and telling you they saw The Grim—it’s simply a behavioral-level analysis that could have been done if someone had followed you around with a video camera your whole life. (It’s actually the case that many disorders have overlapping trait profiles, and thus this isn’t really accurate. But I think you can see my point.) This is really what I mean by saying that a diagnosis is a behavioral thing—saying that “OCD” made you do something is reifying an abstraction. You made you do something. Yeah, maybe it seemed like pulling on the door handle three times is Definitely The Right Number of Times To Pull On A Door Handle™, but you were still the one who did it. And you could have not done it, despite the fact that your brain is telling you that if you don’t do that, your entire family will burn in Turbo Hell forever or something.

Second—am I just coping super hard here? I don’t think I am. If someone asked me if I have ADHD or OCD, I’d probably say no. This is because I don’t really exhibit symptoms of these disorders, and by my personal ontology, this means that I don’t “have” these disorders. But if you really pushed me, I’d tell you that I “used to” have these disorders. I’m not ashamed of this, which is why I’m putting it in a public blog post for everyone to see. And I’m not pigheadedly rejecting a label just for the sake of it. I genuinely think it’s unproductive to view disorders as reified abstractions—the power you gain by viewing them accurately is dramatic. Things like “procrastination” stop being these vague, unchangeable truisms about your character, and start becoming dry, third-person observations you can change. For example, you go from saying things like “I procrastinated on my work for like three hours by going on Instagram because of my ADHD” to “When I go on social media after I get back from school around 5pm and I am tired, I tend to spend too long on it.” The first thing, “procrastination” and “ADHD”, you can’t change. The second? You definitely can. And in fact, this holds for more subtle things as well. Things like “I’m ruminating on my upcoming test because of my OCD” turns into,

“I’m obsessively thinking about my test later because its presence triggers anxious feelings in me. I check and re-check my test preparation to ease my anxiety, and compulsively play out disaster scenarios and what I’d do in response to try and prepare myself for if I do poorly.”

If you’re anything like me, I can come up with pretty plausible solutions to end the behavior of the latter two examples, but I can’t end the former. Ending the former behaviors would require me to vanquish the grand beings of “procrastination” and “OCD”, while the latter are simple behavioral modifications that I can just... do.

Another way you could phrase this is that you are giving up personal power by being vague with your failure modes. Saying that something bad happened “because” of your three-letter disorder is simply false. You may have a greater incidence of unproductive tendencies due to the way your brain chemistry works, but all this is is probabilistic up-weighting of negative options in your purview. You can still choose the positive option. Doing this on a consistent basis will probably require you to structure your life in a way that neurotypical people don’t have to, but this is just something you can do to get the results you want. It’s behavior all the way down.

The final sub-point I’ll make on this second point is that people oftentimes over-ascribe behavior to their disorder rather than themselves. That is, if a person knows they have ADHD, and then they see themselves procrastinate, they will frequently ascribe this to their ADHD rather than just normal human behavior. You could say their “It’s my ADHD!” gun is a bit trigger-happy. The truth is that everyone procrastinates. It’s a normal thing. But the tricky part about being neurodivergent is that you don’t know what it’s like in anyone else’s mind, so you’re liable to see every deficiency in yourself as the consequence of some ethereal disorder rather than the normal human experience. This is obviously a really bad thing, as (a) it’s inaccurate, and (b) it creates a sense of powerlessness in the person experiencing it. I think really the only solution here is to not identify with a disorder in any meaningful sense. You just have to take responsibility for everything in your life and treat it as a behavioral issue. That’s the only way I’ve found to consistently and effectively change the behaviors that I don’t want in my life.

Third, am I advocating for an austere, willpower-based, grindset approach to curing disorders, thus foregoing the more effective treatments of therapy and medication? Kind of, but not really. I think there is no real way that you can consistently mitigate the symptoms of your three-letter disorder if you just try to be a sigma and grind through the pain. At least, I can’t do this. I instead advocate for using systems to change behavior. I, for example, have to implement a strict system to avoid procrastinating like crazy. If I instead tried to not procrastinate by flexing my David Goggins Just Try Harder muscles, I would wildly fail. I know this about myself at this point, and so I instead mitigate my failure modes with a system.

The rough-and-ready definition of a system is that a system is a structure that you put on your life to reduce the amount of willpower you have to use to make good decisions. The idealized version of a system that people should strive for is a system that requires you to use zero willpower to make good decisions. In practice, a “system” really just looks like some sort of schedule or to-do list for the day, along with some habits that you consistently practice. I will give some concrete advice that you can build into your own life later in this post.

I also think that therapy and medication are great! I think you should do what works, and these things work! A good therapist and/or psychiatrist is super useful in helping you get over your three-letter disorder. Talk therapy and CBT are first-line defenses for a number of disorders. And although I can’t speak from experience here, I have heard that medication is a wonderful addition to many people’s lives, especially for things like ADHD and anxiety. I think that building a good system is the crux of improvement for a disorder, and that therapy and medication can greatly help this improvement along.

This also addresses the last point in the steelman above: I think studying these disorders is the only way to fix them permanently, and so psychologists, psychiatrists, and a number of other mental health professionals are integral to this project. By “fix”, I mean that an afflicted person doesn’t have to undergo a system of behavioral modification to achieve normal results—if you “fix” them at the level of their brain chemistry or similar, then they just by default do the normal behavior. I think that as long as AI doesn’t kill everyone in the next few years, these disorders will be a thing of the past by 2035. For example, I think that there will be a “tirzepatide for ADHD” in the next ten years.

Practical methods to get rid of your three-letter disorder

These are some methods that I’ve found useful for reducing various symptoms I have had in the past:

  • ADHD:

    • Keep Do Not Disturb on at all times. This is obvious and I almost forgot to write it. Clearly having notifications interrupt you at all times of the day is not good for your focus.

    • Get rid of social media. Short-form content demolishes your attention span. Try going without it for two weeks to see what I’m talking about. Even long-form content isn’t really safe in my opinion. I’d recommend setting your screentime on these apps and websites to zero minutes per day and then having a friend or partner set the password so you don’t have access to it. You will act like a rabid caged animal to try and get access to social media. You need to make it extremely difficult for you to access it. Do whatever you need to do to block things for yourself. Succeeding in this, however, pays wild dividends, not only for your attention span, but in your overall mental health as well.

    • Single-tasking only. When you do something, just do that thing and nothing else. Don’t scroll social media while you’re talking to someone. Don’t listen to music while studying. Don’t have a conversation with your friend while doing your homework. Just do the thing in front of you and do literally nothing else.

      • Corollary: Do not switch your attention. This is the same thing as multi-tasking but it sounds different. You should organize tasks and your environment in such a way that you do not have to switch your attention basically ever. It dramatically worsens your ability to focus, as by constantly task-switching, you’re literally training yourself to not focus for longer than five seconds. Interestingly, I’ve found that when I was deep in my vibe-coding days, my attention span got noticeably worse—I think this is because I was bouncing between five Claude Code tabs at all times, and thus constantly switching my attention.

    • Control your environment. The ideal working environment is a small room with a single desk, nothing on the walls, no noise, and no other people in it. This is the ideal you should try and aim for when working. Anything that isn’t the work is a distraction. Practically, this means you should try and study in an empty library, or in a room in a library. Some people can study in their room and some can’t. Experiment and figure out what works for you. Something to be critical of: do not confuse loneliness or boredom for a lack of focus. Yes, studying without anyone else around and with no music is more boring than otherwise—grow up.

    • Stick to a schedule. Do everything at around the same time every day. Wake up, go to bed, eat meals, work out, work/study, and do your hygiene on a schedule. The good thing about schedules is that you get to make them, so if you don’t like something, you can just change it!

    • Practice being focused. Who would have thought that actually practicing being focused increases your focus? I’ve noticed that when I get many (4+) hours of consistent deep work in, my brain chemistry changes. I get more focused everywhere by being focused for long periods of time.

    • Exercise and sleep well. These have been proven to dramatically increase quality-of-life, as well as improve ADHD symptoms. They should be your baseline attacks in the war for more focus.

  • OCD

    • Get therapy and learn about the basic tools. This is a two-parter. First, if there are specific life events that you tend to obsess over, talk therapy might be a good idea. Second, a therapist can work with and expose you to OCD-treatment techniques. Some of the basic tools for OCD treatment are (talk to an LLM about these if you want to know more): CBT, ERP ⊆ CBT, meditation (for anxiety reduction), and SSRIs. Talk to Claude or a trained professional to learn more about these.

    • Lower your baseline anxiety. OCD is primarily an anxiety-driven disorder, and so lowering your overall anxiety will likely reduce your OCD—how to reduce anxiety has taken up many textbooks, but the basics are:

      • Exercise

      • Sleep well and keep regular sleep and wake times

      • Have and cultivate good social relationships

      • Do some form of religion or mindfulness practice

      • Have a quality life/romantic partner

      • Simply try to actively reduce your anxiety by, e.g., “un-clenching” or other similar cognitive reframing techniques

    • Be consistent. This is my biggest piece of advice. Just consistently try things, do more of what works, discard what doesn’t work, and be willing to listen to your own advice. Everyone has their own tips and tricks for their own psychology. The sad fact of pre-ASI life is that only you can really find these things out about yourself, and the only way to do it is to try stuff and see what works.

  • Anxiety

    • See above (first two bullet points apply here—just replace “OCD” with “anxiety”). Anxiety is a tricky thing, because the ways to reduce it are actually really obvious and straightforward—you just have to be consistent with them for it to actually work.

Summary

In summary, I believe that you can “get over” your three-letter disorder by implementing a system of behavioral change in your life. Disorders are labels we give to parsimoniously describe bundles of behaviors—they are not abstractions that have “power” over your life. Change your behaviors and the label becomes causally effete. That is, the label can’t predict what you do, and thus there is a real sense in which you do not have the disorder. This advice doesn’t hold for some people, but I would bet that it holds for most. A good system should aim to minimize the amount of willpower you need to make good decisions. A system like this may include the components of therapy and medication. It should definitely include exercise, good sleep, and consistency above all.

Two suggestions for improving automated math and physics research

Really, there are three things that are inhibiting fully automated math and physics research. First, LLMs write like crap and slop out all of the time, making their work basically unreadable. Second, LLMs have no sense of relevance in their work—they think routine, boilerplate steps any undergraduate could do are epistemically identical to brand new, Fields-Medal-level ideas. Third, and this is really a consequence of the second point, LLMs have great trouble simplifying things. If an LLM is explaining something to you, it is likely much more complicated than it needs to be.

I think there are, however, concrete things that we can easily do to negate some of these effects. Some of these are very low-hanging fruit, and actually you can even do some of them right now. I think the first lab that does these things will gain 10s of millions of dollars because of it.

Remark: both of these suggestions are applicable to theoretical AI alignment research as well, and so I think Resolution should do the former on an open-source model to improve the writing of their automated alignment papers. Or they could just use Fulcrum's model.

Do supervised fine-tuning to make LLM writing better.

For the foreseeable future (I predict at least 2 years), humans need to be able to understand model outputs. The problem with this is that models slop out when solving hard problems. Reading their writing is like sticking forks in your eyes while carefully—but ferociously—stubbing your toe on all nearby surfaces. It's chloroform in print (cf. Mark Twain commenting on the Book of Mormon). This makes verifying model outputs extremely difficult and a huge pain in the ass—fixing this issue is ridiculously low-hanging fruit and would produce massive upside. The first company to seriously do this will make 10s of millions because of it, arguably more. People hate slop writing more than you think. The ideal I think one should emulate when doing SFT on writing is Witten. He has the best writing style of any mathematician or physicist and has an incredible amount of output. One might also target Serre, or Scholze. I believe the latter two are harder to emulate because they require more compression than Witten does, which I imagine is very hard for LLMs.

This idea has a proof of concept in the Echo model. Echo is a fine-tuned Kimi K3 model trained to imitate various authors' styles. Interestingly, when the model was fine-tuned on imitating one author, its ability to imitate other authors jumped as well. This may be interpreted as fine-tuning eliciting latent model capabilities rather than training them in from scratch. The punchline of this is that the model's outputs are way more readable. (I'd really recommend trying the model out—it's amazing.) This is also extremely low-hanging fruit—Fulcrum, the people who made Echo, did it for around $5k, and the results are quite good. Doing this on a larger scale would be almost trivial for labs to do, and they'd make probably 10-100x their money on this investment. This is a no-brainer.

(Sidenote: So why, you might ask, have the AI companies not really done this? Is there some high-IQ reason I'm missing here? I think that no, there actually isn't a reason that the AI companies haven't really done this. One reason you might think this is true is because Anthropic released Opus 5. Come on. If they took seriously the idea that slop was a bad thing, let's just say releasing Opus 5 wouldn't have been on the bingo card. Similarly, Astra's outputs are also complete garbage slop. One might try and argue that doing this would decrease reasoning capabilities, but I call bullshit. The best reasoners in human history write beautifully, so it's obviously not contradictory to the best reasoning models writing beautifully. Another thing is that Anthropic must have already done some of this—the difference between Opus 5 and Opus 5.5's slop production is night and day. This means that they can do it again, almost certainly without decreasing reasoning capabilities.)

I will also note that making model outputs more understandable is an incredible move if you're just trying to please users. One of the reasons people love Opus 5.5 so much is because of its writing—it's not perfect, but it's 10x more human than any other model. This is in sharp contrast to Opus 5, whose writing is almost comically slop-filled.

Increase the model's ability to evaluate the importance of something

A key problem with the current models is that they are terrible at judging the relevance of something. This is one of their worst flaws. This is a problem for writing because humans operate on ideas, not prose. In any given paper, there are, like, maybe two new ideas they use. More if it's a good paper, and even more if it's a great paper. The thing is: humans realize this, and they structure their writing accordingly. They spend little, if any, time on boilerplate, and then almost all of their time on the actually new key ideas in their paper. This makes things far easier to learn, as you really only have to remember 2-3 new things (the new ideas), while your background knowledge can fill in the rest of the details. You can usually also get at least a gist of these ideas by just reading the introduction of the paper. Good papers are structured kind of like this:

The model has other plans for you: it's going to do this really fun thing where it doesn't actually tell you the answer to these questions. Enjoy sifting through your slop.

This is an issue of relevance. The model seemingly has little ability to tell what information is genuinely important versus trivial, and its writing reflects its ignorance. There is a sort of constancy of the perceived importance of any given step in an LLM's output, yet this is of course completely false. Multiple Fields medalists have commented on this recently [22:00], [Goal 6.3], and it's one of the biggest inhibitors to both (a) good exposition and (b) automating mathematics research.

Relevance will (almost) certainly be solved with scale and elicitation. It's clear that, in some sense, the models have a superhuman ability to evaluate the relevance of something, as they have solved scores of highly difficult math and physics problems. To do this, they surely have had to evaluate different solution approaches and decide which ones are more important than other ones. I am also sure that in the CoT it should be more or less clear when a critical idea arises. The only question is how to bring this ability to the surface to bear on the writing—and thinking—of the model.

Superintelligent AI and the Problem of Evil

August 25, 2026 at 4:23:55 PM PDT · 5 min read · Substack

Rationalists should realize that God is lot smarter than an artificial superintelligence

We can’t reasonably predict the behavior of superintelligent AI, so why should we assume that we can predict the behavior of an omniscient god?

The rationalist crowd correctly discerns the fact that you cannot reliably reason about the behavior of superintelligent agents. This idea is called “Vingean uncertainty”—the idea that less intelligent agents cannot accurately predict the behavior of far more intelligent ones. If they could predict their behavior, then they’d be just smart as the more intelligent agent, simply by taking all of the predicted actions. An example of this is a chess-playing AI—if I could predict the moves that Stockfish would make, then I could simply make those moves myself, thus being as good as Stockfish at chess.

Vingean uncertainty still allows one to make very general arguments about how superintelligent agents may act. Roughly, the extent of knowledge we have about such agents is isolated in the theses of instrumental convergence and the orthogonality thesis. The first says that goal-driven agents will aim to accumulate power, self-preserve, and not modify their preferences (among other things) in order to achieve their goals most efficaciously. The second says that desires are “orthogonal” to intelligence—that is, you can have an arbitrarily intelligent agent with arbitrarily specified goals. The classic example is a superintelligent AI with its main desire—and hence goal—being to make as many paperclips1 as possible.

The main reason you should be more or less agnostic about the actions of a superintelligent agent is because you’re not that smart. You can imagine that a jumping spider (which has been shown to have basic predictive abilities2) could not reliably predict basically anything about a human actor. It is not unreasonable to assume that this analogy scales to humans predicting the behavior of an ASI. Even more so when the ASI is very smart, similar to something like 100x the gap between a human and an amoeba. Even more so when the being you’re dealing with is infinitely intelligent, or omniscient. This is the situation we are dealing with when we reason about a superintelligent deity. If you’re a rationalist who accepts Vingean uncertainty, you should apply it to your religious credences as well.

In religious philosophy, this problem is called “divine psychology”—the idea that we can’t reliably reason about what God wants to do one way or the other. It is all the rage in modern philosophy of religion3. It more or less destroys the problem of evil and divine hiddenness, but also destroys the fine-tuning argument and psychophysical harmony (among others). The idea behind this is that whatever minimal reasoning you can do about bad things, you can also do about good things. So if you have to discredit this reasoning on one issue, then you also have to discredit it on the other issue. If, for example, I can’t update away from theism4 on the fact that there seems to be gratuitous evil in the world because of divine psychology, then I also can’t update toward theism due to the seemingly absurdly fine-tuned constants of nature. There are rebuttals to this point—namely the Awesome Theistic Evidence argument (and the forthcoming Awesome Atheistic Evidence argument). The idea behind ATE is that it really seems like some things should be evidence of God, even if he is very mysterious and smart—for example, if we somehow found that the initial conditions of the universe spelled out the entire Gospel of John, it seems like that should be pretty strong evidence for Christianity. This is regardless of whatever giga-brained, 13D-chess considerations we have to account for when thinking about the psychology of the deity at hand.

We’ve applied rationalist considerations to philosophy of religion—let’s see if we can now go the other way. Is there an analog of ATE when reasoning about superintelligent AI?

I think there obviously is.

Imagine that, after Anthropic achieves RSI and creates an extremely superintelligent Claude somewhere in the year 2035, we give this agent total freedom of action. Claude then behaves in the following ways: it cures every known disease, spends gargantuan resources on improving human welfare at the seeming expense of its own power, voluntarily builds apparent control and shut-down mechanisms into itself for humans to use, and doesn’t take obvious opportunities to seize power. It does these behaviors for, say, 50 years, until 2085, and does them in every observable environment without any seeming strategic benefit to doing so.

It seems like it would be reasonable to conclude that Claude is probably just aligned to human values. Maybe Claude is secretly deceiving us for some incomprehensibly intelligent giga-brained strategic reason. But if that possibility prevented any update toward Claude being aligned, it seems like behavioral observations can teach us nothing about the true intentions of superintelligent models. That feels too skeptical to me.

Awesome Atheistic Evidence also has some play here. Imagine that instead, Claude starts converting literally everything in sight into paperclips, builds tons of paperclip factories, drains the oceans to supply the coolant for hardening molten paperclips, and starts genociding humans to use their atoms to build paperclips with.

I think it’s pretty reasonable to assume that Claude is now a paperclip maximizer, and thus is not aligned with human values.

This is without regard to the fact that Claude is vastly superintelligent, making the psychology of the model basically inscrutable to us. Even with this gap, we can make prima facie judgements about Claude’s actions when faced with overwhelming confirmatory evidence for a given hypothesis.

If You’re a Quant Intern, You Need to Go into AI Safety

August 24, 2026 at 3:04:07 PM PDT · 6 min read · Substack

Do you really want your life’s work to be trading Peruvian banana futures one microsecond faster?

The smartest technical students at the top schools generally go into one of four career options:

  1. Academia—this means becoming PhD and master’s students in physics, math, or computer science. For example, this is the most common path for MIT math majors1.
  2. Consulting, Investment Banking, or FAANG SWE—reasonably well-paying careers with a relatively high amount of prestige. Quite competitive, but less so compared to the next two options.
  3. AI capabilities research at OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI, etc.—the “new hot job” in town. Promises of massive salaries, very smart coworkers, and the opportunity to bring superintelligence to life.
  4. Quantitative finance—extremely high salaries, genius coworkers, exciting work, and lots of prestige.

To gauge approximately how many of these top students go into quant, about 25% of MIT’s math majors go into finance [1]. From my personal experience, I think it’s reasonable to assume that at least 10% of the top technical students at the best universities go into quantitative finance. The reasons for doing this are pretty simple: you make a lot of money, you have really smart coworkers who are familiar with your social circle2, you get lots of prestige, and the work is interesting.

I posit that if you are a quant intern, you should instead go into AI safety. It’s not hard to see why: you will have the same four elements that make quant work interesting, but you’ll actually be doing something useful with your life. It is completely obvious that quants have basically zero positive impact on the world unless they donate large amounts of their income. Technical AI safety, on the other hand, is easily the most important work any technical person on the planet can do with their life. Plus, you still get almost all of the cool benefits of quantitative finance!

  • Making lots of money. The salary for a technical alignment researcher at an independent organization is around $200k, and at a top independent org (e.g. Redwood, METR, Apollo, FAR.AI, etc.) you make ~$300-800k. If you work at a frontier lab (e.g. OpenAI or Anthropic), you make ~$500k. The latter number is approximately equal to an introductory quant job at a top firm like Jane Street. So, if you’re going to Jane Street for the short-term money, that is no longer a reason to go there comparative to AI safety. The salary for independent labs can be less than introductory quant firm salaries, but not by more than $150k in most cases. This still probably doesn’t matter: more income has dramatically diminishing returns for well-being past ~$100k. Past this number, there is very little reason to optimize for income over other factors in your work. I would actually guess that the reason people care about income past ~$100k is status-signaling—saying that you make a large amount of money feels good, and so people like higher numbers here to flex on their friends and community.
  • Having really smart coworkers. Hard truth: your coworkers likely will not be as talented in AI safety as they would be in quant. This is simply because the field is much smaller and less developed than quantitative finance. It’s similar to how quant was in ~1990-2000—the field was just emerging, so most of the really smart people were in e.g. SWE or more traditional finance roles. However, you will still have lots of talented coworkers, and the more prestigious the lab is the more talented your coworkers are likely to be. I would say you start seeing a high frequency of quant-level talent when you get into the top-level independent labs, e.g. Redwood Research or METR, and this likely only increases at frontier labs. At a frontier lab, the talent levels are most likely the same as top quant firms.
  • Prestige. Unless you work at a frontier lab, this is the main hit you’d take relative to quant. If you go to anyone in a technical major at MIT, they all know what quantitative finance is. Many fewer of them know about AI safety, and about why it’s important. Two arguments for why this shouldn’t really sway you. One, it’s insanely obvious that you should not put random prestige-maxxing tendencies over trying to solve the problem of AI killing everyone on planet Earth. Two, prestige is solved if you just work at a top lab. Saying you work at Anthropic is arguably more sexy than saying you work at Jane Street nowadays. If you can’t live without having a visibly stacked LinkedIn, then work at a frontier lab.
  • Interestingness of the work. Quant work is interesting for two main reasons: you get to play with lots of money and you get to do lots of quick thinking about coding and mathematics. The work is also interesting because you have interesting coworkers, but this was covered in the previous point. Moving lots of money around is fun because it feels important. Doing lots of quick thinking about math and coding is fun because rapidly thinking about technical subjects is fun. I would argue that both of these elements are present in AI safety in varying amounts. First, the work in AI safety is clearly more important than the work in quant: you are working on the problem of keeping literally everyone on the planet Earth from dying. Aligning artificial intelligence is the most important technical problem of the generation, and that’s the problem you get to work on every day. Second, you will be doing less “quick thinking” than in quant, but you will still be doing lots of interesting coding work every day. I would say the work is approximately equal in interesting-ness across quantitative finance and AI safety, with a bias toward quant finance for most people.

In summary, if you’re a quant intern, you should instead go into AI safety. This is for four reasons. First, you will make basically the same amount of money. Second, you will have very interesting coworkers. Third, you will get lots of status. And fourth: the work is interesting. This makes the job itself approximately equal to quantitative finance. AI safety then absolutely topples quant finance when you consider the importance of the work. Therefore, you should work in AI safety.
